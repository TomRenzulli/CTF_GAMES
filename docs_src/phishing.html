An Analyst's Guide to Phishing Emails

Cyber Club Quick Reference · v1.0

Phishing is still the most common way intrusions start, and triaging a reported message is the most common task a junior analyst is handed. The work is methodical, not intuitive: the headers tell you where it came from, the authentication results tell you whether the sender is who they claim, the links and attachments tell you what it wanted. Nothing here requires you to open anything dangerous.

Golden rule: analyze statically, never interactively. Read the raw source, decode attachments on disk, and detonate only in an isolated sandbox. Clicking the link "just to see" confirms your address is live and can burn the investigation.

Do first

Triage
  1. Get the original message — .eml or .msg, not a screenshot or a forward. Forwarding destroys headers.
  2. Open the raw source in a text editor.
  3. Read Received headers bottom-up.
  4. Read Authentication-Results.
  5. Extract every URL and every attachment hash.
  6. Decide: malicious / spam / legitimate, then contain and report.

Never do

Hard stops
  • Never click the link from a corporate or personal machine.
  • Never open the attachment outside an isolated VM with no network.
  • Never reply, and never use the unsubscribe link.
  • Never enable macros — the prompt is the attack.
  • Never forward it normally to colleagues — attach the original instead, or you're phishing your own team.
  • Never paste live URLs into chat. Defang first.

Reading the headers

Every server that handles a message prepends its own Received: line, so the newest hop is at the top and the origin is at the bottom. Read upward to trace the path.

HeaderTrustWhat it tells you
From:NoneFree text chosen by the sender. Display name and address are both trivially forged
Reply-To:NoneWhere a reply actually goes. A mismatch with From: is a strong signal
Return-Path:SomeThe envelope sender — what SPF actually checks, often different from From:
Received: (bottom)PartialEarliest hop — closest to the true origin, but forgeable before it reached you
Received: (yours)HighAdded by your own infrastructure. This is the part that cannot be faked
Message-ID:SomeIts domain should match the sending infrastructure. Often forgotten by the attacker
Authentication-Results:HighYour server's verdict on SPF, DKIM and DMARC

Red flags in a header block

Pattern
  • Originating IP in a country or hosting ASN unrelated to the claimed sender.
  • Message-ID domain ≠ From domain.
  • Reply-To on a different domain — often a lookalike.
  • Display name of a real executive with a free-mail address behind it.
  • A hop count of one — sent directly from a script, not a mail system.
  • Timestamps inconsistent across hops.
  • Missing DKIM-Signature on a domain that normally signs everything.

SPF, DKIM, DMARC — and why alignment is the point

SPF

Is this IP allowed?

A DNS record lists which servers may send for a domain. The receiver checks the connecting IP against it.

Checks the envelope sender, not the From: you see — which is exactly the gap DMARC closes.

DKIM

Was it altered?

The sending domain signs the message with a private key; the receiver verifies with the public key in DNS.

Proves the message came from the signing domain and wasn't modified. Survives forwarding better than SPF.

DMARC

Does it line up?

Requires SPF or DKIM to pass and align with the visible From: domain, then publishes a policy: none, quarantine or reject.

Alignment is the crux — see below.

Why alignment decides it: an attacker can trivially pass SPF for a domain they own — evil.com can publish any SPF record it likes, and the mail still says From: payroll@bnl.gov. What they cannot do is produce an aligned pass for bnl.gov without its DNS or signing key. So spf=pass alone means little; dmarc=fail on a message claiming a real organisation is close to conclusive. Check with dig TXT _dmarc.example.com.

Link analysis

Where it really goes
  • Text ≠ destination. <a href="X">Y</a> shows Y, goes to X. Enumerate every href — real links are often included as decoys.
  • Read the registrable domain — the last two labels before the TLD. login.microsoft.com.evil.ru is evil.ru.
  • Homoglyphs & typos: rn for m, 0 for o, Cyrillic lookalikes (punycode xn--).
  • Open redirects on legitimate domains, and shorteners hiding the target.
  • Long subdomain chains push the real domain off a phone's address bar.
  • Defang before sharing: hxxp://evil[.]com.

Attachment triage

Static first
  • Decode, don't open. Attachments are base64 text inside the message — extract and inspect with zero execution risk.
  • Double extensions: invoice.pdf.bat displays as invoice.pdf because Windows hides known extensions.
  • High-risk types: .bat .exe .js .vbs .lnk .iso .img .hta .scr, and Office files with macros (.docm, .xlsm).
  • Hash it (SHA-256) and check reputation before anything else.
  • Hidden PowerShell (-nop -w hidden -enc) is a near-certain indicator.
  • Password-protected archives exist to defeat scanning, not to protect you.

Reaching a verdict

Then act
  • Malicious — auth fails + deceptive link or weaponized attachment. Block sender/domain/URL, hunt for other recipients and anyone who clicked, reset credentials if any were entered, report to your IR process.
  • Suspicious — mixed signals. Escalate with your evidence; don't release to the user yet.
  • Legitimate — auth aligned, links consistent, context expected. Release and tell the reporter they did the right thing by asking.

Always praise the report. An analyst who makes reporting feel costly gets fewer reports — and the one that goes unreported is the one that lands.

Pressure tactics to recognise

Social engineering
  • Urgency & deadlines — "expires in 4 hours", "account closing today".
  • Authority — impersonating IT, HR, payroll or an executive.
  • Fear — security alert, legal threat, failed payment.
  • Curiosity / reward — bonus letters, parcel notices, shared documents.
  • Secrecy — "don't discuss this with anyone" is the tell in gift-card and wire-transfer fraud.
  • Routine mimicry — the dangerous ones look utterly boring: a quarantine digest, an invoice, a shared file.
Report internally first — your IR team, then national reporting (CISA / NCSC) where applicable
