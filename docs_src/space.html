Space & Satellite Hacking

Cyber Club Quick Reference · v1.0

Satellites feel exotic, but the security story is familiar: protocols designed in an era with no threat model, long-lived hardware that cannot be patched, and a ground network that is just IT infrastructure with a dish attached. The exotic part is the physics — orbits, link budgets and Doppler shift — and the fact that receiving is a normal, legal hobby while transmitting is tightly licensed. Start on the receive side and you can do real work with a $30 radio.

The lesson from every real incident so far: attackers go after the ground segment, not the spacecraft. Modems, terminals, operator workstations and the TT&C network are ordinary computers — and they're the cheap way in.

The four segments

Where the attack surface is
  • Ground segment — mission control, TT&C stations, teleports, scheduling. Normal servers and networks. Highest real-world risk.
  • Space segment — the spacecraft itself: bus, payload, flight software. Hard to reach, impossible to patch casually.
  • Link segment — the RF path up and down. Jamming, spoofing and eavesdropping live here.
  • User segment — terminals, VSATs, GNSS receivers. Enormous installed base, rarely updated.

TT&C = Telemetry, Tracking & Command — the control channel. Compromise it and you command the spacecraft.

Why it's soft

Design-era reality
  • Many downlinks are unencrypted. Weather, telemetry and broadcast payloads are often plaintext by design — and legal to receive.
  • Authentication is frequently absent on older command links; security assumed nobody owned a transmitter.
  • No patching. A 15-year design life and a radiation-hardened CPU means flight software is near-frozen.
  • Long supply chains — bus, payload, ground software and operators are all different vendors.
  • COTS cubesats ship with the same embedded weaknesses as any IoT board.

Orbital basics you actually need

Just enough physics
  • LEO (~200–2000 km) — fast, passes last 5–15 minutes, strong Doppler. Cubesats and imaging.
  • MEO (~20,000 km) — GNSS constellations.
  • GEO (35,786 km) — appears fixed in the sky; point once and forget. TV and traditional satcom.
  • TLE (Two-Line Element) — the orbit description everyone uses. Feed it to tracking software to predict passes. Published openly by CelesTrak / Space-Track.
  • Doppler shift — a LEO pass smears frequency by several kHz; your receiver must track it or the decode fails.
  • Link budget — why antenna, LNA and pointing matter more than software.

Tooling

Receive side
  • RTL-SDR (~$30, receive-only) — the right first purchase. HackRF / LimeSDR / USRP transmit too, which is where licensing applies.
  • GNU Radio — visual DSP flowgraphs; the standard SDR toolkit.
  • gr-satellites — decoders for a hundred-plus real cubesats. The fastest path to a genuine decode.
  • SatDump — turnkey weather-satellite imagery (NOAA, Meteor, GOES).
  • Gpredict / Skyfield (Python) — pass prediction from TLEs.
  • SDR++ / GQRX — general receivers for finding and recording signals.
  • SatNOGS — a global network of ground stations with open observation data. You can analyse real passes without owning hardware.

Practise legally

Start here
  1. Decode NOAA / Meteor weather imagery. Public, unencrypted, designed for open reception. A dipole and an RTL-SDR is enough.
  2. Receive the ISS — APRS digipeater and SSTV events on amateur bands.
  3. Work SatNOGS archives — replay recorded IQ from real passes.
  4. Parse CCSDS by hand — take a frame, strip the headers, extract the APID. Pure binary protocol work, no radio needed.
  5. Hack-A-Sat — the US Space Force CTF; past challenges and solutions are public and are the best structured practice available.
  6. Get a licence if you want to transmit. Amateur radio is the legal route.

Hard legal line

Non-negotiable
  • Receiving public satellite downlinks is legal in most jurisdictions — it is a mainstream hobby.
  • Transmitting without a licence is an offence. Uplinking to a satellite you do not own is a serious federal crime almost everywhere.
  • Never jam or spoof GNSS. Aviation, shipping and emergency services depend on it; even a benchtop test can leak and endanger life.
  • Even where reception is legal, redistributing decoded private content may not be.
  • Keep transmit-capable SDRs on a dummy load or shielded enclosure when testing.
  • Treat intercepted content as sensitive; publish findings, not other people's data.

Incidents worth knowing

  • ViaSat / AcidRain (2022) — wiper malware pushed through the ground management network bricked tens of thousands of modems across Europe at the start of the Ukraine invasion. The canonical "ground segment is the target" case.
  • Hack-A-Sat (2020–) — US Space Force CTF; the 2023 final ran against a real satellite in orbit (Moonlighter). Public challenge archives are excellent study material.
  • GPS jamming & spoofing — routine in conflict zones; has misdirected shipping and disrupted aviation. Civil GNSS has no signal authentication.
  • Iridium pager traffic — researchers demonstrated unencrypted messaging recoverable with commodity SDR hardware.
  • VSAT terminal exposure — internet-facing satellite modems with default credentials have been found across maritime and aviation fleets.
